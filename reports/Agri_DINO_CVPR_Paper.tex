%% ─── Abstract ───────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
General-purpose vision foundation models are strong at broad recognition, but
agricultural images often require decisions from subtle, local cues such as
leaf venation, small lesions, and phenotypic variation across environments.
We introduce \textbf{Agri-DINO}, a DINOv3-initialized Vision Transformer
adapted to agriculture through domain-adaptive pre-training (DAPT) on a
curated corpus of \num{1.52} million high-purity botanical and phytopathological images (audited from a 2M+ candidate pool).
The additional agricultural pre-training uses no downstream human labels.
For downstream transfer, we evaluate a structured protocol that combines
\textbf{two-stage linear probing then fine-tuning (LP-FT)},
\textbf{layer-wise learning-rate decay (LLRD, $\gamma{=}0.75$)}, and
\textbf{$256{\times}256$ tokenization}. The protocol is designed to limit
feature drift on small downstream datasets. On a fixed stratified split of a
fine-grained medicinal-plant classification dataset, Agri-DINO reaches
\textbf{59.67\%} top-1 accuracy, a \textbf{+10.32-point} gain over random
initialization and similar accuracy to supervised ImageNet-21k initialization.
The cumulative ablation quantifies the incremental contribution of each
component. We also diagnose a benchmark-construction error that fragments
disease labels into sample-specific micro-classes and makes the resulting
classification problem appear substantially harder. The study is a controlled
transfer analysis; it does not establish cross-domain robustness or universal
superiority over generic pre-training.
\end{abstract}

%% ─── 1. Introduction ────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

Plant diseases and pests destroy an estimated \$220 billion of the global
agricultural harvest annually~\cite{savary2019global}, while access to expert
plant pathologists remains limited. Visual diagnosis from smartphones, field
cameras, and drones could help, but only if models can resolve fine-grained
signals such as early chlorosis, marginal necrosis, and spore-colony morphology
under changing illumination, viewpoint, and background conditions.

The dominant transfer-learning recipe in agricultural vision is to initialize
a convolutional or transformer backbone on ImageNet and fine-tune it on a
small, task-specific plant dataset. This recipe is useful, but it leaves two
gaps. First, ImageNet's visual statistics are only an imperfect match for plant
tissue and field acquisition. Second, supervised pre-training requires large
labeled corpora, whereas botanical and phytopathological annotation requires
specialized expertise~\cite{mohanty2016using}. Self-supervision removes the
label requirement for pre-training, but it does not remove the adaptation
problem: small downstream datasets can induce feature drift, and the standard
$224\times224$ input can undersample fine biological structure.

\noindent\textbf{This work} studies these issues through the following
components of Agri-DINO:

\noindent\textbf{C1. DINOv3-Initialized Agricultural Adaptation.}
Starting from an official DINOv3 ViT-Base/16 checkpoint, we stream
\num{1.52} million curated RGB images—systematically audited from 28+ public repositories to eliminate duplicate archives, $128{\times}128$ satellite tiles, and evaluation data leakage—through a DINO-style student--teacher
distillation pipeline. The resulting 86M-parameter
\texttt{ViT-Base/16} checkpoint is adapted to agricultural visual statistics
without using the source labels.

\noindent\textbf{C2. Two-Stage LP-FT Adaptation Protocol.}
We evaluate a two-stage protocol: a brief linear-probing warmup to align the
classification head, followed by backbone fine-tuning with layer-wise learning
rate decay. In the cumulative ablation, the full protocol is 24.48 points above
the no-LP, low-resolution, no-TTA baseline; this sequence is not an independent
estimate of forgetting or of any single component's effect.

\noindent\textbf{C3. Evaluation Protocol and Benchmark Audit.}
We define standardized train/validation splits and evaluation scripts for
downstream benchmarking, and we expose a micro-class fragmentation pathology in
one YOLO-to-classification conversion that can distort the apparent task
difficulty. We use ``Agri-Bench'' as shorthand for this protocol and audit, not
as a claim of a fully released multi-task benchmark suite.

On the reported medicinal-plant split, Agri-DINO performs similarly to
supervised ImageNet-21k initialization under the reported adaptation protocols,
without using downstream human labels during additional pre-training. We treat
this result as evidence for the value of in-domain self-supervision on this
split, not as a claim that one benchmark establishes general agricultural
superiority.

%% ─── 2. Related Work ────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Agricultural Recognition and Benchmarks}
Plant recognition is a fine-grained, context-sensitive problem: class identity
may depend on small morphological cues or distributed symptoms, while images
vary with crop stage, organ, illumination, viewpoint, and field background
\cite{picek2022plant,barbedo2022representativeness}. The commonly used
benchmarks cover different regimes. PlantVillage is a controlled, single-leaf
disease benchmark~\cite{hughes2016open}, whereas iNaturalist and PlantCLEF
emphasize large-vocabulary species recognition in more natural imagery
\cite{vanhorn2018inaturalist,goeau2022plantclef}. Their scores are therefore
not interchangeable: the benchmarks differ in capture conditions, taxonomy,
label quality, and even the unit of prediction. Dataset reviews also identify
inconsistent splits, annotation noise, and leakage through related images as
sources of optimistic estimates~\cite{xu2024datasets,noyan2022bias}. We make
these protocol choices explicit and treat PlantSeg as a data-quality audit.

\subsection{Self-Supervised Pre-Training}
Self-supervised learning (SSL) removes the need for human labels during
pre-training. Contrastive methods such as SimCLR~\cite{chen2020simple} learn
agreement between augmented views while separating other images. Masked image
modeling (MIM) methods such as BEiT~\cite{bao2022beit} and MAE~\cite{he2022masked}
instead predict masked tokens or pixels, which is attractive for learning local
structure and dense representations. DINO~\cite{caron2021emerging} uses a
momentum teacher and centered cross-view predictions; its features exhibit
semantic neighborhoods and spatial organization without an explicit
negative-pair queue. iBOT~\cite{zhou2022ibot} adds masked-patch prediction,
while DINOv2~\cite{oquab2024dinov2} scales the recipe through careful curation
and self-distillation. Our backbone is initialized from the official
DINOv3~\cite{simeoni2025dinov3} ViT-Base/16 checkpoint before the additional
agricultural adaptation.

We choose DINO as the primary objective because the downstream task is
fine-grained classification: semantic separability and local-neighborhood
structure are more directly aligned with the target than reconstruction fidelity
alone. This is a task-motivated choice, not a claim that DINO dominates every
SSL setting. Our corpus is roughly 27 times smaller than LVD-142M, so coverage
of agricultural acquisition conditions is an important complement to scale.

\subsection{Domain Adaptation}
Domain-aligned pre-training has improved transfer in specialized domains such
as pathology and remote sensing, where acquisition physics, field of view, and
cross-scene variation differ from generic web imagery~\cite{kang2023pathology,peng2022domain}.
Agricultural studies point in the same direction. AgriNet and PDDD-PreTrain
show that plant-domain representations can improve disease and pest transfer
over generic initialization~\cite{sahili2022agrinet,dong2023pddd}; unsupervised
adaptation and agricultural foundation models further show that unlabeled
in-domain images can help bridge laboratory-to-field shifts
\cite{wu2023field,nahian2025agrifm}. Evidence remains fragmented across crops,
tasks, and protocols, and few studies isolate self-supervised domain adaptation
from downstream fine-tuning on a fixed backbone. Agri-DINO addresses this
narrower, controlled question.

\subsection{ViT Fine-Tuning}
End-to-end fine-tuning of a large ViT on a small dataset can overfit and distort
useful pre-trained features~\cite{kumar2022fine}. LP-FT first fits the
classification head with the backbone frozen, then permits controlled backbone
updates; the staged optimization reduces the early feature drift caused by a
randomly initialized head. Layer-wise learning-rate decay (LLRD) complements
this schedule by assigning smaller updates to earlier blocks. Parameter-efficient
alternatives such as BitFit~\cite{ben2022bitfit} and low-rank updates can reduce
memory and storage, and can preserve pre-training-domain performance in some
settings~\cite{bafghi2024peft}, but add method-specific design choices. We study
LP-FT with LLRD because it preserves full adaptation while remaining simple to
inspect in the cumulative ablation; the goal is transfer robustness, not a PEFT
comparison.

\subsection{Benchmarking and Positioning}
Benchmark design is part of the scientific problem. VTAB~\cite{zhai2019large}
shows that representation quality depends on the task suite, and agricultural
benchmarks add further variation in class granularity, split strategy,
preprocessing, and label quality. We therefore report the split, taxonomy, and
input protocol explicitly and include a deterministic evaluation harness. Our
contribution is a controlled joint study of DINO-based domain-adaptive
pre-training, LP-FT/LLRD adaptation, and benchmark construction on a common
ViT-Base/16 backbone—not a claim of universal agricultural superiority.

%% --- 3.1 Pre-Training Corpus Construction ---
\subsection{Pre-Training Corpus Audit and Scale Normalization}
\label{sec:corpus}

\noindent\textbf{Multi-Source Candidate Audit and Deduplication.}
We construct our pre-training pool from 28 publicly available agricultural and botanical datasets (\texttt{Dataset.yml}), initially totaling over 2.07~million raw candidate images (926.9~GB). An audit of uncurated agricultural repositories reveals three systematic data pathologies that severely degrade self-supervised Vision Transformers: (i)~\emph{duplicate and leakage contamination}, wherein mirrored ZIP archives (e.g., duplicate releases of \texttt{Plant Disease Expert} and \texttt{Plant Leaves}) account for 15.9~GB of redundant tensors; (ii)~\emph{out-of-domain spatial resolution}, exemplified by \texttt{GeoPlant} (188,407 satellite patches at $128{\times}128$ px, 53\% grayscale), which falls below the standard $224{\times}224$ crop window and forces zero-padded border reconstruction; and (iii)~\emph{downstream benchmark leakage}, where evaluation targets overlap with pre-training sources. We strictly remove all duplicate archives, discard sub-224~px satellite patches and tiny datasets ($<200$ samples), and exclude all downstream evaluation benchmarks (\texttt{MedicinalPlant}, \texttt{PlantSeg}, \texttt{Edible Wild Plants}, \texttt{Pea Plant}, \texttt{Indian Medicinal Plants}) to guarantee zero data leakage.

\noindent\textbf{Curated Taxonomy and Domain Distribution.}
After deduplication and filtering, our curated pre-training corpus comprises \textbf{\num{1516428} high-purity RGB images} sharded into streaming WebDataset \texttt{.tar} archives. The corpus is structured across four complementary agricultural domains:
(1)~\emph{Foliar Pathology and Disease Lesions} ($\approx 70\%$, e.g., \texttt{PlantVillage} [162k], \texttt{PlantifyDr} [125k], \texttt{Plant Disease Expert} [200k], \texttt{Wheat Plant Diseases} [16k], \texttt{Ghana Crop Disease} [50k]), providing fine-grained chlorosis, necrosis, and rust patterns;
(2)~\emph{Botanical Biodiversity and Taxonomy} ($\approx 20\%$, e.g., \texttt{PlantNet-300K} across 1,081 species, \texttt{Plants Leafs} [190k], \texttt{House Plant Species} [30k]), capturing diverse morphological structures;
(3)~\emph{Weed and Seedling Discrimination} ($\approx 7\%$, e.g., \texttt{DeepWeeds} [17.5k], \texttt{Soybean Weed Detection} [30.6k], \texttt{CottonWeedDet3} [20k], \texttt{V2 Plant Seedlings} [11k]), providing in-field background clutter and occlusion; and
(4)~\emph{Canopy Phenotyping and Aerial Surveys} ($\approx 3\%$, e.g., \texttt{OPPD} [47k], \texttt{UNL-CPPD} [50k], \texttt{Agriculture-Vision} [94k]), supplying macro-scale field structure.

\noindent\textbf{Bounded Multi-Scale Cropping.}
A second pathology in agricultural vision is extreme scale variance: candidate resolutions range from $31{\times}31$~px thumbnails to $6000{\times}4000$~px aerial field scans (\texttt{OPPD}, \texttt{CottonWeedDet3}), with 63.3\% of images falling either below 224~px or above 4096~px. Under naive ImageNet random cropping, a $224{\times}224$ crop on a 4096~px scan samples only 0.3\% of the image area—frequently capturing empty soil background. Conversely, 1-channel grayscale datasets (e.g., 60\% of \texttt{Cotton Plant Disease}) broadcast zero-chrominance RGB tensors. We resolve these issues by introducing a \textbf{Bounded Multi-Scale Crop} pipeline during WebDataset generation: any image with minimum side $>1024$~px is first rescaled to a 1024~px bounding frame, small images ($<224$~px) are bicubically upscaled, and 1-channel images are converted to true RGB. When DINO multi-crop augmentation (two global views at $0.4\text{--}1.0$ scale and four local views at $0.05\text{--}0.4$ scale) is applied to these bounded frames, every crop consistently samples semantically meaningful plant tissue.

%% --- 3.2 Self-Supervised Pre-Training ---
\subsection{Self-Supervised Pre-Training Objective}
\label{sec:pretrain}

Let $\mathcal{D} = \{x_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$ denote the unlabeled pre-training
corpus. We instantiate a student network with parameters $\theta_s$ and a
momentum teacher with parameters $\theta_t$, initialized from the same official
DINOv3-pre-trained ViT-Base/16 checkpoint~\cite{simeoni2025dinov3}. This
initialization makes our method an additional agricultural adaptation stage
rather than a from-scratch pre-training run. For each image $x$, the augmentation
pipeline produces two global views $V_g = \{v_1^g, v_2^g\}$ (covering at least
50\% of the image area) and four local views $V_l$ (covering
$20\text{--}50\%$). The student processes all views, while the teacher processes
only the global views. We minimize the cross-entropy between the teacher and
student output distributions:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DINO}}
  = \sum_{v \in V_g \cup V_l}
    \sum_{\substack{v' \in V_g \\ v' \neq v}}
    H\!\left(P_t(v'),\, P_s(v)\right),
  \label{eq:dino}
\end{equation}
where $H(a,b) = -a\log b$ is the cross-entropy and $P_s,\, P_t$ are
softmax-normalized projection-head outputs. We center the teacher outputs before
the softmax, following the DINO training recipe. Teacher parameters are updated
with an exponential moving average:
\begin{equation}
  \theta_t \;\leftarrow\; \lambda\,\theta_t + (1-\lambda)\,\theta_s,
  \quad \lambda \in [0.996,\, 1.0],
  \label{eq:ema}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ follows a cosine schedule that approaches 1 over training.

%% --- 3.3 Two-Stage LP-FT ---
\subsection{Two-Stage LP-FT with Domain-Calibrated LLRD}
\label{sec:lpft}

\paragraph{Motivation.}
When a self-supervised ViT is fine-tuned end-to-end on a small downstream
dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{down}}$, the randomly initialized classification
head can produce large early gradients before it has aligned with the frozen
feature space. Such updates can distort pre-trained features and reduce
out-of-distribution robustness~\cite{kumar2022fine}. We therefore separate head
alignment from backbone adaptation. In our cumulative ablation, the full
protocol is 24.48 points above the no-LP, low-resolution, no-TTA baseline; this
difference includes several interventions and is not interpreted as a direct
measurement of forgetting.

\paragraph{Stage 1: Linear Probing (Epochs $1 \leq e \leq E_w$).}
The backbone parameters $\theta_{\mathrm{bb}}$ are strictly frozen:
\begin{equation}
  \nabla_{\theta_{\mathrm{bb}}}\mathcal{L} = \mathbf{0},
  \quad e \leq E_w = 5.
  \label{eq:freeze}
\end{equation}
Only $W_{\mathrm{head}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.02^2)$ is updated via AdamW.
This warmup aligns the classification hyperplane with the frozen DINO feature
space before any gradient signal reaches the backbone.

\paragraph{Stage 2: End-to-End LLRD Fine-Tuning ($e > E_w$).}
The backbone is unfrozen and Layer-wise Learning Rate Decay (LLRD)
is applied. For transformer block $l \in \{1,\ldots,L\}$ (with
$L = 12$ for ViT-Base), the effective learning rate is:
\begin{equation}
  \eta_l = \eta_{\mathrm{base}} \cdot \gamma^{\,L+1-l},
  \label{eq:llrd}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma = 0.75$ is the decay factor used in our experiments. This assigns
\emph{lower} learning rates to early blocks and \emph{higher} rates to upper
blocks. We use this value as a practical setting for the present benchmark; a
broader sweep is needed before treating it as a universal calibration for
self-supervised features.

%% --- 3.4 High-Resolution Tokenization & TTA ---
\subsection{High-Resolution Tokenization and Test-Time Augmentation}
\label{sec:resolution}

Botanical species discrimination can depend on fine structural cues---leaf
margin serrations, trichome density, and vein branching patterns---that are
spatially undersampled at the standard $224\times224$ resolution
($N_p = 196$ patches). We therefore use $256\times256$ inputs, increasing the
sequence length to $N_p = 256$ patches; the ViT positional embeddings are
interpolated to this grid.

At inference, we apply \textbf{test-time augmentation (TTA)} by averaging the
pre-softmax logits for the original image and its horizontal reflection:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}
  = \arg\max\!\left[
    \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(f_\theta(x) + f_\theta(\mathrm{flip}(x))\bigr)
  \right].
  \label{eq:tta}
\end{equation}
We treat the horizontal flip as label-preserving for the image-level taxonomy
used here. This assumption should be revisited for tasks where orientation itself
is diagnostic.

%% ─── 4. Experiments ─────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Experiments}

We organize the evaluation around four reader-facing questions: how Agri-DINO
compares with standard initialization, which adaptation components matter, how
the training dynamics evolve, and whether the PlantSeg benchmark is well-formed.

\subsection{Datasets and Implementation}

\noindent\textbf{Downstream datasets.}
We evaluate on two benchmarks:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{MedicinalPlant:} 1,301 images across 30 medicinal
        plant species; fine-grained leaf and stem morphology.
        80\%/20\% train/validation split stratified by class.
  \item \textbf{PlantSeg:} Crop disease images originally distributed
        as oriented bounding-box (OBB) YOLO annotations, converted to
        an image-classification layout. We report a fragmented-label audit and
        analyze a systematic labeling pathology (\S\ref{sec:plantseg}); the
        corrected taxonomy is not yet evaluated.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Implementation details.}
All experiments run on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 (24 GB VRAM) under
PyTorch 2.0 with \texttt{bfloat16} mixed precision. We use the AdamW
optimizer with base learning rate $\eta = 3\!\times\!10^{-4}$, weight
decay 0.1, label smoothing 0.2, batch size 128, and 50 training epochs.
A cosine annealing schedule governs $\eta$ after a 5-epoch warmup.
Gradient clipping at norm 1.0 and dropout (rate 0.1) are applied throughout.
For LP-FT runs, only the head is trained for the first 5 epochs;
the full model is trained thereafter with LLRD ($\gamma = 0.75$).

\subsection{Analysis: Micro-Class Fragmentation in PlantSeg}
\label{sec:plantseg}

All three initialization methods achieve only $\approx 2.6\%$ accuracy on

the conversion pipeline and found a systematic benchmark pathology.

The automated conversion pipeline parsed sample-level file identifiers (e.g.,
\texttt{banana-black-sigatoka-10-} and \texttt{banana-black-sigatoka-102-}) as
distinct taxonomic classes, inflating the label space from the true
\textbf{20} disease categories to \textbf{300 micro-classes}. With only a few
hundred images, most classes then contain fewer than three training examples.
The random-chance baseline is $100/300 \approx 0.33\%$; the observed
2.42--2.78\% is only modestly informative given this fragmentation. We are
correcting the pipeline by stripping numeric suffixes before taxonomic
assignment, but corrected-taxonomy results are not yet available. More broadly,
any conversion from YOLO annotations to image-classification folders should
separate instance identifiers from semantic labels explicitly.

%% ─── 5. Discussion ──────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}

\noindent\textbf{In-domain self-supervision.}
On the reported \texttt{MedicinalPlant} split, Agri-DINO (59.67\%) is 0.32
points below supervised ImageNet-21k initialization (59.99\%), despite using
no downstream human labels during additional pre-training. This result suggests
that unlabeled agricultural images can provide a competitive initialization when
labeled botanical data are scarce. The comparison is protocol-dependent and
should not be extrapolated to field robustness or to other taxonomies without
external and group-disjoint evaluation.

\noindent\textbf{Why LP-FT helps in this setting.}
The LP warmup contributes \textbf{+12.59 points} in our cumulative ablation, the
largest single increment. A plausible explanation is that the randomly
initialized head is initially mismatched to DINO's view-invariant feature
space; fitting the head first reduces that mismatch before backbone updates
begin. This interpretation is consistent with the ablation, but it is not a
direct measurement of feature drift or a universal property of all
self-supervised backbones.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations and future directions.}
Four limitations bound the current conclusions. First, evaluation is limited to
image classification on one medicinal-plant split; it does not test lesion
segmentation, oriented detection, counting, or cross-farm generalization.
Second, the reported comparison uses one split and one run, so the 0.32-point
difference to ImageNet-21k should not be treated as statistically meaningful
without repeated trials. Third, all experiments use RGB images. Near-infrared
and related spectral channels can expose early stress signals, motivating a
lightweight adapter for multispectral input. Fourth, $256\!\times\!256$ remains
modest for small lesions, while scaling toward $448\!\times\!448$ increases
quadratic attention cost. Future work should combine repeated, leakage-safe
evaluation with higher resolution, real multispectral data, and external-domain
tests.

%% ─── 6. Conclusion ──────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

We presented Agri-DINO, a DINOv3-initialized Vision Transformer adapted to
fine-grained plant recognition with additional agricultural self-supervision and
a two-stage LP-FT protocol. On one stratified medicinal-plant split, the
reported Agri-DINO run improves accuracy by \textbf{+10.32 points} over random
initialization and is close to the ImageNet-21k baseline under different
adaptation protocols. The cumulative ablation identifies the incremental gains
from resolution, LP warmup, LLRD, and TTA, while the PlantSeg audit exposes an
annotation-conversion error that fragments semantic labels into sample-specific
classes. These results motivate broader evaluation, not a claim of universal
agricultural superiority. Repeated seeds, leakage-safe splits, corpus-overlap
audits, corrected PlantSeg labels, and external-domain tests are needed to
establish how far the observed gains generalize.

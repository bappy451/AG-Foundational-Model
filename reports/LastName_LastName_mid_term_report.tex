\begin{abstract}
This project investigates continual self-supervised pretraining of official
Vision Transformer (ViT) and Yolo checkpoints on a diverse agricultural image corpus.
Our aim is to improve transfer across crops, diseases, acquisition settings, and
sensor modalities without substantially changing the pretrained backbone. Since
the proposal, we have built a single training stack that supports ViT-S/B/L
models initialized from ImageNet checkpoints; masked
image modeling (MIM); DINO-style student--teacher training; and a learnable
$1\!\times\!1$ spectral adapter for RGB and multiband inputs. The data pipeline
can stream ordinary folders, ZIP and nested-ZIP archives, GeoTIFFs, NumPy arrays,
and WebDataset shards. The validated catalog now contains 5,175,016 RGB images,
up from 412,454 images at proposal time. We have verified end-to-end MIM and
DINO training, five-band inputs, and a 474-epoch SparK--YOLO run whose best
validation loss fell by 18.46\%. These are encouraging engineering results, but
they do not yet demonstrate better representations. Real multispectral data,
duplicate control, source-balanced sampling, and held-out-domain comparisons are
still being completed. The next phase will set ImageNet baselines and compare
linear-probe, few-shot, and fine-tuning performance before larger runs begin.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Agricultural imagery varies in ways that standard image benchmarks often do not
capture. A crop can look different across growth stages, stress conditions,
seasons, locations, management practices, cameras, and lighting. Disease
benchmarks frequently contain centered leaf images, while practical systems must
handle cluttered field scenes, unmanned aerial vehicle imagery, or multispectral
rasters. Labels are also expensive to collect and are scattered across tasks such
as species identification, disease diagnosis, weed recognition, detection,
segmentation, counting, and temporal monitoring. A model that performs well on
a random split of one dataset may therefore fail to transfer to a new farm or a
different sensor.

We ask whether continual self-supervised pretraining can adapt an official ViT
to agricultural visual structure while retaining the benefits of generic
pretraining. The study compares reconstruction-based MIM with view-invariant
DINO-style self-distillation and will also test a sequential
MIM$\rightarrow$DINO schedule. A lightweight spectral adapter maps any number
of aligned input bands to the three channels expected by an official RGB ViT.
Keeping the backbone fixed makes the effect of sensor adaptation easier to
measure.

At this midpoint, the project has a working research infrastructure and a much
larger indexed corpus. The software supports both objectives, several official
checkpoint families, archive-scale streaming, reproducible runs, and downstream
task runners. The central scientific question, however, remains open. Passing
tests and decreasing pretext losses show that the pipeline works; they do not
show that it transfers better. For that reason, we separate engineering evidence
from scientific results and defer performance claims until controlled downstream
experiments are complete.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\textbf{Vision Transformers.} ViT treats an image as a sequence of patch tokens
processed by a Transformer encoder. With sufficiently large-scale pretraining,
this approach can match strong convolutional models~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}.
We use official ViT-S, ViT-B, and ViT-L implementations rather than a custom
transformer so that initialization and model scale remain controlled across
experiments.

\textbf{Masked image modeling.} MAE hides a large fraction of image patches and
trains an asymmetric encoder--decoder to reconstruct them, yielding transferable
features~\cite{he2022mae}. Other masked-image methods similarly show that
reconstruction or token prediction can provide a useful self-supervised
signal~\cite{bao2022beit}. SparK applies the same broad idea to convolutional
encoders through a sparse hierarchy~\cite{tian2023spark}. Agricultural lesions,
leaf venation, edges, and canopy texture make this local reconstruction signal
worth testing for domain adaptation.

\textbf{Self-distillation.} DINO trains a student to match the centered,
sharpened outputs of an exponential-moving-average (EMA) teacher from different
augmented views~\cite{caron2021dino}. DINOv2 showed that careful curation and
self-distillation can produce robust visual features without labels~\cite{oquab2024dinov2}. Because reconstruction and self-distillation emphasize
different invariances, we evaluate MIM-only, DINO-only, and sequential training
with the same data and backbone stack.

\textbf{Agricultural transfer and spectral adaptation.} PlantVillage helped
establish deep learning for image-based plant-disease recognition, but its
controlled backgrounds also highlight the gap between benchmark and field
conditions~\cite{hughes2015plantvillage,mohanty2016using}. Remote-sensing models
such as SatMAE extend masked pretraining to temporal and multispectral satellite
imagery~\cite{cong2022satmae}. Our approach instead keeps an official RGB
backbone and learns a small sensor-specific projection. This gives us a direct
comparison with RGB-only input and with patch-embedding expansion.

\section{Approach}
\subsection{Data and Sampling}
The proposal described 412,454 images from 13 downloaded datasets. The validated
catalog now indexes 5,175,016 images visible to the loaders, stored across about
1~TB of ZIP and TAR archives. It includes plant species, disease, seedling, weed,
and crop imagery, although nearly all samples are still RGB. Each record retains
a stable sample key, source identifier, and portable archive-member path. For
large sources, we create WebDataset shards of roughly 1~GB after RGB conversion,
bounded resizing, and validation. The preprocessing workers handle one archive
at a time, which avoids repeated archive opens and expensive seeks.

To keep a single large collection from dominating training, the planned sampler
will choose a source first and a sample second. When source metadata permits,
validation splits will be group-disjoint. Before the final experiments, we will
freeze the manifest, record dataset versions and licenses, and remove exact and
near-duplicate clusters before defining training and held-out-domain partitions.

\subsection{Sensor-Adaptive Backbone}
For an input $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}$, a learnable
$1\!\times\!1$ convolution projects the $C$ sensor bands to the three channels
expected by an official pretrained ViT:
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{z}_{h,w}=A\mathbf{x}_{h,w}+\mathbf{b},\qquad
 A\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times C}.
 \label{eq:adapter}
\end{equation}
For RGB, $A$ is initialized to the $3\times3$ identity and $\mathbf{b}=0$.
For multiband input, available RGB-like channels initialize the corresponding
outputs; the additional-band weights start at zero but can be learned. We then
normalize the adapted image using the statistics for the selected checkpoint and
feed it to the unchanged ViT patch embedding. The adapter adds just $3C+3$
parameters, separating sensor mixing from spatial feature learning.

\subsection{Self-Supervised Objectives}
In MIM, we mask a random set $\mathcal{M}$ containing 75\% of the patch tokens.
A linear reconstruction head predicts the adapted RGB patch $\mathbf{p}_i$ from
the encoded token $\mathbf{h}_i$, and the loss is evaluated only at masked
positions:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MIM}}=
 \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}
 \left\|g(\mathbf{h}_i)-\mathbf{p}_i\right\|_2^2.
 \label{eq:mim}
\end{equation}

For DINO-style training, the student receives global and local crops while the
teacher receives paired global crops. With student temperature $\tau_s$, teacher
temperature $\tau_t$, and running center $\mathbf{c}$, the cross-view loss is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DINO}}=-\sum_k
 q_t^{(k)}\log q_s^{(k)},\quad
 q_t=\mathrm{softmax}((\mathbf{o}_t-\mathbf{c})/\tau_t).
 \label{eq:dino}
\end{equation}
The teacher parameters---adapter, backbone, and projection head---are updated by
$\theta_t\leftarrow m\theta_t+(1-m)\theta_s$. The implementation supports
ImageNet, MAE, DINOv2, and DINOv3 initialization, as well as an explicit
MIM$\rightarrow$DINO handoff that begins a new optimization run from a compatible
MIM checkpoint.

\subsection{Training and Evaluation Protocol}
Training uses BF16 mixed precision, gradient accumulation, optional gradient
checkpointing, cosine learning-rate schedules, and multi-process data loading.
Each run saves the resolved configuration, environment and Git metadata,
metrics, figures, best and latest checkpoints, optimizer state, and
random-number-generator state. WebDataset handles CPU streaming, while NVIDIA
DALI is available on Linux for GPU JPEG decoding. Development began on a single
RTX~4090; the next stage will use Auburn's Slurm cluster for longer controlled
runs.

The evaluation will compare the unadapted official checkpoint with RGB-only
continual pretraining, MIM, DINO, sequential MIM$\rightarrow$DINO, frozen and
trainable adapters, and direct patch-embedding expansion. We will report
accuracy and macro-F1 for classification; mAP, IoU/Dice, and MAE/RMSE will be
used for detection, segmentation, and counting as appropriate. Linear probes,
few-shot subsets, and full fine-tuning will each be repeated with at least three
seeds. The main test sets will hold out entire sources, crops, geographic areas,
seasons, or sensors, and shared splits will include means, standard deviations,
and paired comparisons.

\subsection{Long-Run SparK--YOLO Pretraining}
We also evaluated the convolutional branch with a 500-epoch SparK run using the
WebDataset pipeline and a YOLO11-L backbone. We initialized from the official
YOLO checkpoint, inserted $1\!\times\!1$ expansion bridges where the dense and
sparse channel dimensions differed, and transferred 215 tensors to the sparse
encoder. Each epoch contained 4,000 training batches. The learning rate warmed
from $2\!\times\!10^{-6}$ to $10^{-5}$ during the first five epochs, stayed near
that value early in the run, and then decayed toward $10^{-6}$ on a cosine
schedule.

When we analyzed the run, 474 epochs had completed and epoch 475 was 1,232 of
4,000 batches in progress. Validation loss decreased from 0.8071 at epoch 1 to
0.6581 at epoch 472, a relative reduction of 18.46\%. Training loss at that
point was 0.6524, for a gap of 0.0057. Table~\ref{tab:spark-run} lists selected
checkpoints. Most of the improvement arrived in the first 100 epochs. After
that, the curve improved more slowly but still reached new minima at epochs 168,
257, 297, 353, 411, 427, 452, and 472. Across the final 25 completed epochs,
validation loss averaged 0.6610 with a standard deviation of 0.0014. This tight
range suggests a stable late-stage plateau rather than divergence. The final
observed validation loss (0.6615) was slightly above the minimum, so subsequent
experiments will use the saved best checkpoint rather than the terminal state.

The automated suite covers RGB, NPY, GeoTIFF, ZIP, and nested-ZIP loading;
group-disjoint splits; NoData handling; padding for undersized images; official
model selection; adapter initialization; MIM and DINO tensor behavior; EMA
updates; paired-crop replay; strict configuration parsing; atomic checkpointing;
and RoPE-based DINOv3 backbones. Recent shard-builder tests also check parallel
archive grouping and valid TAR output. Together, these tests reduce the chance
that a long pretraining run fails because of data-format, checkpoint, or model-
geometry errors.

\subsection{Interpretation and Remaining Gaps}
The expanded corpus, working pipelines, and four-day SparK--YOLO run meet the
mid-term engineering milestone: the planned models can now train reproducibly at
archive scale. The long run gives useful evidence that sparse conversion,
transferred YOLO weights, checkpoint selection, and WebDataset streaming remain
numerically stable beyond a smoke test. It does not show that the representation
improves a recognition task, since reconstruction loss can decrease without
improving transfer.

The same official backbone also accepts RGB and five-band tensors without a
replacement patch embedding, which supports the feasibility of the spectral
interface. It does \emph{not} tell us whether extra bands improve predictions.
The five-band inputs used for verification are synthetic, and the catalog still
lacks a verified real multispectral archive.

No model has yet outperformed its official ImageNet or DINO initialization on a
held-out agricultural benchmark. Accordingly, we do not claim improved accuracy,
robustness, or label efficiency at this stage. The main risks are duplicate
leakage, imbalance from the 306k-image PlantNet subset and other large sources,
RGB-only bias, and I/O saturation during multi-GPU training. We will address the
first three through manifest freezing, duplicate clustering, source-balanced
sampling, and external domain splits. Before requesting more GPUs, we will
measure the remaining risk by comparing CPU WebDataset and DALI throughput.

The next milestone is a ViT-S experiment matrix on frozen data: official
initialization, MIM, DINO, and sequential MIM$\rightarrow$DINO. We will then run
linear probes and low-label fine-tuning on source-disjoint disease and species
benchmarks. Only approaches that improve transfer consistently across seeds will
move on to ViT-B. In parallel, we will add a licensed real multispectral source
and compare frozen and trainable adapters with direct patch-embedding expansion.
This sequence keeps the conclusions tied to the evidence and to the data that
are actually available.
